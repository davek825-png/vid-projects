# The Road That Disappeared Every Winter

A letter does not weigh very much. Until somebody has to carry it across a mountain.

\[beat]

Every winter, the road over the Sierra Nevada mountain range disappeared. Not for a day or two. For months.

Snow piled up in drifts so deep that a wagon stopped being a practical idea, and a horse could sink out of sight.

On the Nevada side of the mountains, in Carson Valley, people waited for word from the west. Their mail came from Sacramento, on the far side of the range. In winter, the mail stopped.

Then, in 1855, a notice ran in the Sacramento Union. The headline was blunt: "People Lost to the World. Uncle Sam Needs a Mail Carrier."

That is an unusually candid employment advertisement.

The employment ad identified the customer. It identified the problem. It didn't identify how a mail carrier would get the job done in the snowy winter months, over tall mountain passes.

The Placerville, California postmaster had a problem. Letters were stacking up in his office. The carrier who had been hauling them over the mountains had vanished, and the postmaster could find no one to take his place.

The Sierra Nevada stood between the two communities. Winter snow cut off the routes that carried supplies, business, and news from home. Those who had tried to cross the Sierra with pack mules and regular snowshoes had failed.

For a person waiting on the other side, the distance was not simply a matter of miles. It was a matter of not knowing about the people they loved.

\[beat]

A Norwegian immigrant named John Thompson came into the post office to ask about the job. He was about twenty-eight years old. He had come to California to dig for gold, and the gold had not paid off. He wanted the mail job.

The postmaster told him the situation plainly. The contract was not his to give. He had no authority to hire Thompson, and no authority to pay him.

One account says Thompson came back every morning for five days until the postmaster finally said yes.

\[beat]

Over time, Thompson earned the nickname "Snowshoe Thompson", but his actual equipment is where the nickname goes wrong.

People called him Snowshoe, but he did not carry snowshoes, the woven kind most people pictured. He used long wooden boards, about ten feet long, weighing roughly twenty-five pounds each. The Sacramento paper called them Norwegian skates. Today, we call them skis.

\[beat]

He also carried a single long pole. It helped him push, balance, and slow himself when going downhill.

The method was different from walking across the snow. It allowed him to slide over it.

That did not remove the climb. It did not shorten the distance. And it did not make the mail carry itself.

But it gave him a workable way to cover the roughly ninety miles between Placerville and Carson Valley, in what was then Utah Territory and is now Nevada.

\[lightly] So the nickname was a misnomer. Snowshoes are built to stay on top of the snow. Thompson's boards were built to slide across it.

\[beat]

Then there was the load.

He carried sixty to eighty pounds of letters and packages, sometimes more. He carried little food. He melted snow for water. He went without a blanket, because a blanket would have taken room the mail needed.

The crossing took about three days going east, and about two coming back. On some days he covered close to forty-five miles.

\[slower]

\[beat]

Late in 1856, on one of those crossings, Thompson came upon a prospector named James Sisson. Sisson was alone in a cabin south of Lake Tahoe. He had been there for twelve days, living on raw flour, with no fire. A January 1857 Sacramento newspaper account describes the scene: Sisson lying on the floor, with nothing but his clothes and his boots, frozen to his feet.

Thompson went to Genoa and came back with a team of men and a sled. They hauled Sisson out through the drifts.

The doctor in Genoa said Sisson's feet would need to be amputated. There was no chloroform in town.

Whatever his reasons, Thompson set out again. He went back over the mountains, got chloroform, and returned. A 2005 public television investigation says he arrived just in time.

Thompson got a freezing man out of the snow.

\[beat]

For twenty winters, from 1856 to 1876, he carried that mail over the Sierra Nevada mountains.

The letters arrived. The people in Carson Valley got their news. The road disappeared every winter, but the mail still arrived.

\[beat]

In 1869, the Nevada Legislature asked Congress to pay Thompson six thousand dollars for his service. Congress did not pay.

Three years later, in 1872, Thompson went to Washington himself to make the case for being paid to do the job. He was told that since he did not have a written contract to carry the mail, he had no claim for pay.

He had never signed one.

He is often quoted as saying that if he did his job and got the mail through, Uncle Sam would pay him.

\[beat]

Thompson died in 1876, at the age of forty-nine. Accounts say appendicitis, which may have developed into pneumonia.

\[slower]

Ironically, a bill to approve his payment was in Congress when he died. Accounts say the bill died with him The mail got through for 20 years. He carried news from loved ones, enabled business transactions by mail, and got a freezing man out of the snow, and he never received payment for any of it.
